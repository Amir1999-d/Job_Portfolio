01 / MSc thesis

Beyond the Weekday

An Open-Data Framework for Holiday Transit Equity, with an Application to Milan.

Public transport is commonly assessed against weekday service. The thesis studies the reduced service run on public holidays, a period that is rarely analysed, and asks three questions:

  1. RQ1How is scheduled holiday service distributed across populations with different levels of potential transit dependence?
  2. RQ2Where does a high holiday service burden coincide with poor walk-up access to essential services?
  3. RQ3How far could a fixed quantity of modelled service reduce the disparity, and how much of that gain survives the operator’s mode and depot structure?

The core framework combines Azienda Trasporti Milanesi (ATM) scheduled timetables in General Transit Feed Specification (GTFS) format, 2021 census sections from Italy’s national statistical institute (ISTAT), and OpenStreetMap essential destinations. It studies eight public holidays across four seasonal feeds. Seven enter the cross-holiday ranking; Labour Day is excluded from that ranking because the relevant feed has anomalous stop coverage.

The method is built to be transferable first and then applied to Milan. Operator-provided metro entries and a line-to-depot register support later checks and the depot extension; they are not inputs to the open-data core, so the core is designed to be rerun in other cities that publish GTFS and small-area census data. So far it has been applied only to Milan.

02 / Method

One framework.
Five phases.

The procedure separates the reusable analytical steps from the local choices needed to run them in Milan. Inputs, thresholds and derived outputs are recorded with each run in a reproducible Python–GIS pipeline.

GTFS: scheduled service and calendars ISTAT: small-area demographic composition OpenStreetMap: essential destinations
  1. 01

    Map vulnerability

    Fit population-share indicators across 6,055 populated census sections: older residents (40%), foreign residents (30%) and single-person households (30%). Attach the composite index to stops.

  2. 02

    Measure scheduled supply

    Reconstruct day types, derive stop-level headways and calculate a vulnerability-weighted burden.

  3. 03

    Compare individual holidays

    Separate recurring patterns from date-specific exceptions and inspect feed-coverage anomalies.

  4. 04

    Test essential access

    Cross holiday burden with category-specific walk-up access to food retail, pharmacies and healthcare to identify double exclusion.

  5. 05

    Bound a reallocation

    Reassign equal modelled stop-service equivalents between donor and recipient stops; compare equity outcomes.

Operational extension / ATM data

Apply bus-and-tram eligibility, departure-weighted stop-to-line attribution and depot-local pools. This layer requires the operator’s registry; it tests structural compatibility, not timetable feasibility.

03 / What changes under constraints

The benchmark becomes a bound.

At the Moderate scenario’s nominal 20% donor reduction, the stop-level model moves 10,015 equivalents per day and improves the Gini coefficient of social burden by 20.8%. Successive constraints reduce the modelled gain.

All bars refer to the same Moderate scenario. Values are improvements in the Gini coefficient, expressed as a percentage.

The practical question

How much of the theoretical gain survives the network structure?

The strict depot rule retains 48.9% of the benchmark Gini improvement; the assumed cross-depot allowance retains 69.6%. These are shares of the equity gain, not shares of service that can be scheduled.

Under the strict rule, 7,530 transferable equivalents are freed, 5,571 placed within their supplying depots and 1,959 returned to donors. Route continuity, vehicles and duties still require operator-led design.

04 / Robustness and limits

What the results can and cannot carry.

Each headline figure depends on modelling choices. These are the tests run and the boundaries stated in the thesis.

Sensitivity test

Vulnerability weights

The index was re-run across 36 alternative weight vectors. Burden rankings stayed strongly correlated with the adopted specification (minimum Spearman ρ = 0.856), while threshold-defined target sets showed greater membership sensitivity. The rankings are stable; the exact priority list is less so.

Unit of analysis

Scheduled supply, not experience

Burden is measured from timetabled headways at stop level. The thesis does not measure actual waiting times, ridership or achieved accessibility; mapped proximity to essential destinations is a proxy.

Reallocation

Model units, not vehicle trips

Reallocated service is counted in stop-service equivalents within a fixed modelling budget. The depot screen tests structural compatibility; it is not a feasible timetable, vehicle schedule or crew plan.

External check

Consistency, not validation

The metro-entry comparison (r = −0.01 across 108 stations) is used as an external consistency check on supply retention. It is not a demand model and does not validate the burden index.

05 / Research directions

Questions I want to pursue.

The thesis ends where its data ends. Each question below starts from a stated limitation and names the method it would need. Answering them takes statistical models of observed service and travel, and mathematical programming for scheduling. Those methods go beyond my MSc, and learning them is what I want from a PhD.

Public transport planning and operations Accessibility and equity Service design and resource allocation Demand-responsive transport GTFS-based network analysis Spatial and GIS methods Data-driven urban mobility

  1. Does delivered service widen the scheduled gap?

    The thesis measures the timetable only. Compare scheduled with delivered headways from automatic vehicle location or GTFS-Realtime records, and test whether reliability losses on thin-service days fall harder on transit-dependent areas.

    Data: AVL / GTFS-Realtime · Method: statistical models of headway deviation

  2. Can an equity gain be scheduled?

    Formulate the reallocation as an optimisation problem, with an equity objective subject to fleet, depot, route-continuity and crew constraints. Solve it at line level and compare the result with the stop-level bound reported above.

    Method: mathematical programming · Benchmark: the 20.8% → 10.2% bound

  3. Does scheduled burden predict who travels?

    The metro-entry comparison (r = −0.01) was only a consistency check. Link holiday burden to ridership or smart-card data to test whether thin service is associated with fewer trips from transit-dependent areas.

    Data: smart-card or ridership records · Method: demand models

  4. Are the gaps persistent, and for whom?

    Extend the framework to several years of feeds to separate structural disadvantage from one-year timetable choices, and replace the three-indicator relative index with a more direct measure of transit dependence.

    Data: multi-year GTFS panel · Method: index validation
